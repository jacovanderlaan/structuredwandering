Count, Don't Rate

A star rating asks a stranger how they felt. Your photo count records what you did. Only one of those survives contact with your own memory.

By Jaco & Annemarie · 2026-07-23

Everyone rates. Four stars for the restaurant, five for the view, three "would not return" for the museum. Ratings are how the travel world decides what's worth your time — an average of how strangers felt, on days you weren't there, about a version of the place that may no longer exist.

We stopped trusting that number. Not because it lies, but because there's a better one sitting in our own pocket: how many photos a place pulled out of us.

Attention leaves a trail

You don't decide to take a lot of photos somewhere. It happens to you. A place holds your attention and your hand keeps lifting the phone; a place doesn't and it doesn't. By the time you're home, the count is already recorded, honest, and completely unedited by the story you'll later tell about the trip.

When we lined up our own trips by photo count per place, the pattern was almost embarrassing in how clear it was:

  • Pont du Galetas, where the Verdon river meets the lake — 102 photos in a single stop.
  • Orto Botanico di Palermo — 106 on one afternoon.
  • A roadside spot in the Provence gorges we'd never have named in advance — dozens, unprompted.

And the inverse, quieter but just as useful: the "must-see" stops we'd dutifully routed toward that pulled three or four frames and a polite nod. We rated those places highly in advance. Our cameras rated them honestly on the day.

A rating is a feeling someone reports. A count is a thing you did. When they disagree, believe the thing you did.

Why the count is harder to fool

A star rating is a summary written after the fact, filtered through mood, price, weather, and the very human urge to justify the trip you paid for. It's a story about an experience.

A photo count is a byproduct. Nobody games their own camera roll. You weren't trying to prove anything to yourself when you took the eightieth shot of the light on the water — you just couldn't stop. That's what makes the number trustworthy: it was never meant to be a measurement. It's engagement caught in the act, before judgment got involved.

This is the same instinct behind everything we do here: prefer the signal people leave while doing a thing over the signal they produce about it afterward. Behaviour over review. The trail over the testimonial.

How to use it

You don't need software. Open last year's trip and sort, roughly, by where the photos cluster.

  • The clusters are your real highlights. Not the ones you'd list from memory — the ones your hand voted for. That's your "go back" list.
  • The thin spots are your "skip it" list. A place you barely photographed, however well-reviewed, didn't hold you. Trust that next time.
  • Weigh it by time. A hundred photos over ten minutes is a fireworks moment; a hundred over a slow afternoon is a place that kept giving. Both matter, differently.
  • Then, and only then, read the reviews — to explain why a place held you, not to decide whether it did. You already know that part.

The quiet version of a big idea

We think the best trips are chosen, not scrolled. Counting your own photos is the smallest, most personal way to choose: it turns a pile of images into a ranked, honest verdict on where your own attention actually went — a verdict no aggregate of strangers can give you, about the only traveller whose taste you're trying to serve.

Rate less. Count more. Your attention already told the truth; all you have to do is add it up.
