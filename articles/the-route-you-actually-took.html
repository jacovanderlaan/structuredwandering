Deliberate travel

The Route You Actually Took

We planned rafting. The photos say we spent the day standing at the overlooks. Your camera roll is the most honest travel journal you own — you just can't read it yet.

By Jaco & Annemarie · 2026-07-23

Before a trip, we plan. We read the guides, mark the sights, build the days. After a trip, we remember — softly, selectively, the way memory works. And somewhere between the plan and the memory sits a third version that's more accurate than either: where we actually stood, when, and for how long. It's recorded automatically, on every phone, and almost nobody reads it.

We decided to read ours.

The plan is a hypothesis

Take our week in the Gorges du Verdon. The plan was active: rafting one day, a kayak the next, the Sentier Martel — the famous canyon hike — on the third. Good intentions, written down in advance.

Then we pulled the location trail out of the week's photos and laid it over the map. The rafting doesn't appear. Neither does the kayak, nor the Martel. What the trail shows instead is two long days at Rougon — the village at the lip of the Point Sublime, the most famous overlook of the gorge — then Moustiers-Sainte-Marie, then the hill village of Bargème, then a slow loop through the red gorges to a col at 1,809 metres.

We didn't raft. We stood at the edges and looked. And here's the thing: that was the better trip. The plan was a hypothesis about what we'd enjoy; the photos are the result of the experiment. When they disagree, the photos win — because we voted with our feet and our shutter, not with our intentions the month before.

Density is a vote

Once the route is honest, a second signal appears: how many photos each place earned.

This isn't vanity data. A place where we took eighty photos and a place where we took three are telling us something we didn't consciously decide. On our Sicily trip, one afternoon at the Orto Botanico in Palermo drew more than a hundred frames; a dozen "must-see" stops nearby drew a handful each. On the drive through Provence, a single roadside belvédère — not on any itinerary — pulled more attention than a château we'd specifically routed toward.

Photo density is engagement you can't fake and didn't plan. It's the closest thing we have to an objective answer to the only question that matters after the fact: was this place worth it? Reviews tell you what strangers felt. Density tells you what you did, before you had a chance to tidy the story.

The plan says where you meant to go. The photos say where you couldn't stop looking. Curate from the second one.

Why this is the whole method, in miniature

Structured Wandering has always argued that the best trips are chosen, not scrolled — that structure and taste beat the firehose of generic advice. Reading your own routes is that argument turned inward. You stop trusting the marketing and you stop trusting your own tidy memory. You trust the record.

And the record compounds. Do it once and you have a corrected map of one trip. Do it across every trip and you have something no guidebook can sell you: a personal atlas of the places that, for you specifically, earned the time. Not the places that photograph well for someone else's feed — the places your week bent toward when no one was optimizing it.

That atlas is the honest foundation under a recommendation. When we say a place is worth the detour, we'd like to mean it the way the data means it: not because a source ranked it, but because our own route, unprompted, kept returning to it.

How to read your own trail

You don't need our system to start. The signal is already on your phone.

  • The photos carry the place. Every shot has a time and, usually, a location. That's the route — not the one you planned, the one you walked.
  • Let the map name the places, not your memory. A timeline of stops will tell you the café, the overlook, the village — often more precisely than you'd recall a week later.
  • Count, don't just look. Where the photos pile up is where the trip actually happened. That's your shortlist for "again" and your warning for "skip next time."
  • Compare it to the plan. The gap between the two is the most useful travel lesson you'll get — it's you, telling you what you actually like.

This was never only about travel

The plan is what we tell ourselves we'll value. The route is what we valued. Most of life has that same gap, and most of us only ever see the first half of it. Travel just happens to leave an unusually clean trail — timestamped, geotagged, sitting in your pocket — of the difference between the two.

Read the trail. It's kinder and more accurate than your memory, and it's already written.
